case study · deterministic finite automata

A real-life DFA: the automated turnstile gate

Picture the electronic control behind a turnstile at a metro station or amusement park: it starts Locked, and every passenger action moves it to exactly one next state — never two, never none.

  • Pushing the barrier while locked keeps it Locked (rejected, alarm sounds).
  • Tapping a valid coin or ticket switches it to Unlocked.
  • Tapping another coin while already unlocked keeps it Unlocked (the extra fare is absorbed).
  • Pushing through the unlocked barrier rotates it and snaps straight back to Locked.

The gate never has to remember how many coins came in or how many people already passed — only its current state and the next symbol. That's what makes it a clean DFA rather than something needing memory: finite states, one deterministic move per input, done.

case study · cryptanalysis & computing history

The Bombe (1939): automating cryptanalysis in World War II

The Bombe was an electromechanical decryption device designed by Alan Turing in 1939 at Bletchley Park in the United Kingdom. Developed during World War II, the machine was built specifically to break the daily encryption keys used by the German Enigma cipher machine and allow Allied intelligence to decipher secret enemy communications at scale.

The Bombe design

The Bombe was designed as an automated search tool rather than a standard calculator. It recreated the internal circuitry of multiple Enigma machines working simultaneously. Important features of the design included:

  • Electromechanical drums — rotating metal drums that simulated the stepping rotors of several Enigma machines connected together.
  • Crib-based processing — designed to test logical chains based on a "crib," a short piece of guessed plain text (such as routine weather terms).
  • Automated logical contradiction elimination — current passed through internal circuits to rule out impossible key combinations instantly.
  • The Diagonal Board — an enhancement added by mathematician Gordon Welchman that drastically expanded the machine's ability to eliminate invalid settings.
  • Automatic stop mechanism — the machine automatically stopped spinning whenever it found a settings combination that contained no logical contradictions.

Operation and scale

The first prototype machine, named Victory, began operating in March 1940. Over the course of the war, Bletchley Park and secondary sites built and operated over 200 Bombes. The machines were primarily maintained and run by members of the Women's Royal Naval Service (Wrens).

Why the Bombe matters

The Bombe project is significant in history because it turned complex, time-consuming cryptanalysis into an automated industrial process. By testing thousands of daily rotor settings in hours rather than weeks, it provided vital military intelligence (code-named Ultra) that helped protect Allied naval convoys and shortened World War II by years.
